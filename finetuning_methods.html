Fine-Tuning & Adaptation Methods:
From Full Updates to Parameter-Efficient Tuning

Domain-adaptive pretraining, full fine-tuning, LoRA, QLoRA, adapters, prefix tuning, knowledge distillation, contrastive fine-tuning, instruction tuning, RLHF/DPO, model compression, and the practical details that interviews test — every method an Applied Scientist needs, with math, diagrams, and 15 interview questions.

1. Why Fine-Tune at All?

Modern ML follows a two-phase paradigm: pretrain on massive general data, then fine-tune on task-specific data. Pretraining learns general representations — syntax, world knowledge, visual features. Fine-tuning adapts those representations to your specific task — ranking connector documents, classifying sentiment, generating code.

Figure 1: The two-phase paradigm. Pretraining is expensive and done once; fine-tuning is cheap and done per task. The key question: how much of the pretrained model do you need to update?

1.1 When to Fine-Tune vs. Prompt

Not every task needs fine-tuning. The decision depends on how far your task is from the pretraining distribution, how much labeled data you have, and your latency/cost constraints:

ApproachWhen to UseData NeededCost
Zero/few-shot promptingTask is close to pretraining; prototyping0–100 examplesLowest
Parameter-efficient (LoRA)Moderate domain shift; limited compute1K–100K examplesLow
Full fine-tuningLarge domain shift; ample data + compute10K+ examplesHigh

1.2 Catastrophic Forgetting

The fundamental tension in fine-tuning: gradient updates that improve task performance can overwrite the general representations learned during pretraining. A model fine-tuned on legal text might lose its ability to reason about code. This is catastrophic forgetting.

Every adaptation method navigates this tradeoff differently. Full fine-tuning updates everything (maximum expressiveness, maximum forgetting risk). Parameter-efficient methods constrain updates to a small subspace (less forgetting, slightly less expressiveness). Understanding this tension is the thread connecting every method in this article.

2. Domain-Adaptive Pretraining (DAPT)

📄
Don't Stop Pretraining: Adapt Language Models to Domains and Tasks Gururangan et al. · ACL 2020 — Showed continued pretraining on domain data consistently helps

Before task-specific fine-tuning, there's often a valuable intermediate step: continue pretraining on unlabeled domain-specific text. A general model trained on web text doesn't understand the vocabulary, jargon, and reasoning patterns of your target domain. DAPT bridges this gap cheaply — you don't need labels, just raw text from the domain.

Figure: The three-phase pipeline. DAPT sits between general pretraining and task fine-tuning, adapting the model's representations to the target domain using the same pretraining objective (MLM or CLM) but on domain-specific text.

Examples: BioBERT (continued pretraining on PubMed), SciBERT (scientific papers), LegalBERT (legal corpora). Gururangan et al. showed DAPT helps consistently across domains — even when the domain seems "close" to general pretraining, continued pretraining on domain text improves downstream performance.

TAPT (Task-Adaptive Pretraining): Even narrower — continue pretraining on the unlabeled task data itself (e.g., the documents you'll be searching over). Surprisingly effective even with small corpora, because it adapts the model to the exact distribution it will encounter at inference.

💡 When to use DAPT vs. skip to fine-tuning

Use DAPT when your target domain has specialized vocabulary, conventions, or reasoning that general pretraining wouldn't cover well — legal, medical, scientific, enterprise IT. Skip it when the domain is already well-represented in pretraining data (general web content, common Q&A). DAPT is essentially free if you have domain text — no labels needed, just compute time for continued pretraining.

3. Full Fine-Tuning

The baseline approach: initialize from pretrained weights, then update all parameters with gradient descent on your task-specific loss.

Full Fine-Tuning Update
$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \cdot \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(x, y; \theta_t)$$ All \(N\) parameters are updated at each step. For a 7B model, this means 7 billion gradients computed and applied per batch.

2.1 When Full Fine-Tuning Works

  • Sufficient data — enough examples to update all parameters without overfitting (typically 50K+ for large models)
  • Large domain shift — the target domain is far from pretraining (e.g., medical, legal, or a new language)
  • Sufficient compute — you can afford the memory for full optimizer states (Adam stores 2 copies of all parameters as moments)

2.2 Memory Cost

This is why full fine-tuning of 7B+ models requires multiple A100 80GB GPUs. For a 70B model, you need distributed training across a cluster. Parameter-efficient methods exist precisely because this cost is prohibitive for most teams.

2.3 Learning Rate Considerations

💡 Why fine-tuning uses 10–100× smaller learning rates than pretraining

During pretraining, weights are initialized randomly and need large updates to reach a good basin. During fine-tuning, you're already in a good basin — the pretrained weights encode useful representations. A large learning rate would catapult you out of this basin, destroying what was learned. Typical fine-tuning LR: 1e-5 to 5e-5 (vs. 1e-3 to 3e-4 for pretraining).

2.4 Separate Copy Per Task

Full fine-tuning produces a completely new model for each task. If you have 10 tasks, you store 10 copies of the full model — 70 GB × 10 = 700 GB for a 7B model. This is where parameter-efficient methods shine: LoRA adds ~0.1–1% extra parameters per task, so 10 tasks ≈ 10 small adapters + 1 shared base model.

4. Feature Extraction & Linear Probing

The opposite extreme from full fine-tuning: freeze the entire pretrained model and train only a new classification head on top.

Figure 2: Linear probing. The pretrained backbone produces fixed features; only the classification head is trained. Zero forgetting risk, but limited by the quality of pretrained features for your specific task.

Why this works: pretrained representations are already rich. A BERT model's [CLS] token or a ViT's output embedding already encodes useful features — the head just learns the mapping to your labels.

When it fails: when pretrained features don't capture the distinctions your task needs. A model pretrained on web text might not produce features that distinguish connector ticket priorities, because that distinction didn't appear in pretraining data.

3.1 Progressive Unfreezing

📄
Universal Language Model Fine-tuning for Text Classification (ULMFiT) Howard & Ruder · ACL 2018 — Introduced progressive unfreezing, discriminative LR, and slanted triangular LR

ULMFiT proposed a middle ground: start with linear probing (only the head), then gradually unfreeze layers from top to bottom, with lower layers getting smaller learning rates. The insight: top layers encode task-specific features (easy to adapt), while bottom layers encode general features (should change minimally).

Discriminative Learning Rates
$$\eta_l = \eta_{\text{base}} \cdot \alpha^{L-l}$$ where \(l\) is the layer index, \(L\) is total layers, and \(\alpha < 1\) (typically 0.5 or 2.6⁻¹). Layer 1 (bottom) gets the smallest LR; the top layer gets \(\eta_{\text{base}}\).

This was an important precursor to parameter-efficient methods — it showed that you don't need to update all parameters equally, and that constraining lower-layer updates helps preserve pretrained knowledge.

5. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)

📄
LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models Hu et al. · ICLR 2022 — The foundational parameter-efficient fine-tuning method

LoRA is the most widely used parameter-efficient fine-tuning method. The core insight: the weight updates during fine-tuning have low intrinsic rank. Instead of updating the full weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\), decompose the update into two small matrices:

LoRA Decomposition
$$W' = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + BA$$ where \(W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is frozen, \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\), \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}\), and \(r \ll d\).

For \(d = 4096\) and \(r = 16\): full update = 16.7M params, LoRA update = 131K params (128× reduction).
Figure 3: LoRA forward pass. Input x flows through both the frozen pretrained weight W₀ and the low-rank adapter BA. Outputs are summed. During training, gradients only flow through A and B. At inference, BA can be merged into W₀ for zero additional latency.

4.1 Why Low-Rank Works

📄
Intrinsic Dimensionality Explains the Effectiveness of Language Model Fine-Tuning Aghajanyan et al. · ACL 2021 — Showed pretrained models have low intrinsic dimensionality

Aghajanyan et al. demonstrated that pretrained models can be fine-tuned effectively in a subspace with dimension far smaller than the total parameter count. For a model with millions of parameters, the meaningful optimization happens in a subspace of a few thousand dimensions. This is the theoretical justification for LoRA — if the update lives in a low-dimensional subspace, a low-rank decomposition can capture it.

Intuitively: pretraining already places the model near a good solution. Fine-tuning only needs to make a small adjustment — and small adjustments in high-dimensional spaces tend to be low-rank.

4.2 Initialization

💡 Why B is initialized to zero

\(A\) is initialized with random Gaussian values, while \(B\) is initialized to zero. This means \(\Delta W = BA = 0\) at the start of training — the model begins at exactly the pretrained weights. Training then gradually moves the update away from zero. This is crucial: random initialization of both A and B would perturb the pretrained weights immediately, potentially causing unstable early training.

4.3 Which Layers to Apply LoRA To

The original paper tested applying LoRA to different attention projections in GPT-3:

Target ModulesParamsPerformance
WQ onlyLowestGood
WQ + WVLowBest tradeoff
WQ + WK + WV + WOMediumSlightly better
All attention + FFNHigherBest (diminishing returns)

WQ and WV is the most common default — it gives the best parameter-efficiency tradeoff. Applying LoRA to FFN layers adds more parameters and can help when the task requires learning new factual associations (since FFN layers act as key-value memories for facts), but the gains are often marginal.

4.4 Rank Selection

Rank \(r\) controls the capacity of the adapter. Higher rank = more parameters = more expressive, but with diminishing returns:

Figure 4: Task performance vs. LoRA rank. Performance improves steeply from r=1 to r=8–16, then plateaus. The sweet spot (r=8 to 16) captures most of the benefit at a fraction of the parameters. For very complex tasks or large domain shifts, r=32–64 can help.

Rules of thumb: r=8 for simple classification/adaptation, r=16 for moderate complexity, r=32–64 for complex tasks or when matching full fine-tuning quality. Always validate with a sweep if time permits.

4.5 The Alpha Parameter

LoRA Scaling
$$h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot BAx$$ The ratio \(\alpha/r\) controls how strongly the adapter influences the output. Typically \(\alpha = 2r\) or \(\alpha = r\), so the effective scaling is 1–2. Higher \(\alpha\) means stronger adaptation; lower means the model stays closer to pretrained behavior.

When you increase rank, you typically keep alpha proportional so the per-parameter contribution stays similar. This is why HuggingFace PEFT defaults to lora_alpha = 2 * lora_r.

4.6 Merging at Inference

The killer advantage of LoRA over adapters: at inference time, compute \(W' = W_0 + BA\) once and replace the original weight. The model now has zero additional latency — it's architecturally identical to the original, just with different weights. No extra layers, no extra computation.

This also enables multi-task serving: keep one base model in GPU memory, store task-specific LoRA adapters (a few MB each) on disk, and swap them in per request by adding the appropriate \(BA\) to the base weights.

6. QLoRA

📄
QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized Language Models Dettmers et al. · NeurIPS 2023 — Fine-tune 65B models on a single 48GB GPU

QLoRA combines 4-bit quantization of the base model with LoRA adapters in full precision. The result: fine-tune a 65B parameter model on a single 48GB GPU with no observable quality loss compared to full 16-bit fine-tuning.

5.1 Three Innovations

1. 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization. Pretrained weight distributions are approximately normal (centered around zero, bell-shaped). NF4 places quantization levels at the quantiles of the normal distribution — equal number of weights per bin — rather than spacing them uniformly (INT4). This gives optimal information-theoretic quantization for normally distributed data.

Figure 5: INT4 vs NF4 quantization. INT4 spaces bins uniformly, wasting precision in the sparse tails. NF4 places bins at normal distribution quantiles — each bin holds the same number of weights, minimizing quantization error where it matters most.

2. Double quantization. Each block of 64 weights shares a quantization constant (scale factor). These constants themselves take 32 bits each — adding up. Double quantization quantizes the quantization constants to 8 bits, saving an additional ~0.37 bits per parameter.

3. Paged optimizers. Uses NVIDIA unified memory to automatically page optimizer states between GPU and CPU memory during gradient checkpointing. This prevents out-of-memory crashes during training spikes without manual memory management.

5.2 The Compute Path

Figure 6: QLoRA compute path. The base model is stored in NF4 (4-bit), dequantized to BF16 on-the-fly for the forward pass. Gradients only flow through the LoRA adapters (BF16). The 4-bit base model is never updated.
⚠️ A common misconception

"Doesn't 4-bit quantization lose too much information?" — You're not training the quantized weights. The quantized base model is a frozen feature extractor. All learning happens in the full-precision LoRA adapters. The quantization error in the base model is a fixed approximation that the adapters can compensate for. Empirically, QLoRA matches 16-bit LoRA performance on standard benchmarks.

5.3 Memory Comparison

Method7B Model13B Model65B Model

QLoRA made it possible for researchers with a single consumer GPU to fine-tune models that previously required a cluster. This democratized large-model fine-tuning and is the basis for most open-source fine-tuning (Alpaca, Vicuna, etc.).

7. Adapter Layers

📄
Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning for NLP Houlsby et al. · ICML 2019 — Introduced adapter modules for transformers

Before LoRA, adapters were the leading parameter-efficient approach. The idea: insert small bottleneck modules after each attention and FFN sub-layer. Only the adapter parameters are trained; everything else is frozen.

Figure 7: Adapter insertion in a transformer layer. Two adapter modules are inserted — one after attention, one after FFN. Each adapter has a down-projection, nonlinearity, and up-projection, plus a residual connection (omitted for clarity). Only the blue adapter parameters are trained.
Adapter Forward Pass
$$\text{Adapter}(h) = h + f(hW_{\text{down}})W_{\text{up}}$$ where \(W_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}\), \(W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}\), \(m \ll d\) is the bottleneck dimension, and \(f\) is a nonlinearity (ReLU or GELU). The residual \(h + \ldots\) ensures the adapter starts as an identity function if \(W_{\text{up}}\) is initialized near zero.

6.1 Adapters vs. LoRA

PropertyAdaptersLoRA
Architecture changeAdds new sequential layersModifies existing weights
Inference latencyAdds latency (extra layers in forward pass)Zero added latency (merge into weights)
MergingCannot merge into base modelCan merge: W' = W₀ + BA
Multi-taskSwap adapter modules per taskSwap small matrices per task
Parameter efficiency~3.6% of original params~0.1–1% of original params

LoRA's ability to merge into the base weights at inference — eliminating any added latency — is the main reason it has largely replaced adapters in practice. Adapters are still relevant as historical context and appear in some multi-modal architectures (e.g., LLaVA uses a linear adapter to project vision features into the LLM's input space).

8. Prefix Tuning & Prompt Tuning

Instead of modifying weights, these methods modify the input by prepending learnable continuous vectors — "virtual tokens" — that steer the model's behavior.

7.1 Prefix Tuning

📄
Prefix-Tuning: Optimizing Continuous Prompts for Generation Li & Liang · ACL 2021 — Learnable prefixes at every transformer layer

Prefix tuning prepends \(l\) learnable vectors to the key and value matrices at every layer. The model attends to these virtual tokens as if they were part of the input sequence, but they encode task-specific instructions in continuous embedding space.

Prefix Tuning
$$K = [K_{\text{prefix}}; K_{\text{input}}], \quad V = [V_{\text{prefix}}; V_{\text{input}}]$$ where \(K_{\text{prefix}}, V_{\text{prefix}} \in \mathbb{R}^{l \times d}\) are learnable at every layer.

For a model with \(L\) layers and prefix length \(l\): total trainable params = \(2 \times L \times l \times d\).

7.2 Prompt Tuning

📄
The Power of Scale for Parameter-Efficient Prompt Tuning Lester et al. · EMNLP 2021 — Soft prompts at the input layer only

A simpler version: only prepend learnable embeddings at the input layer, not at every layer. These "soft prompts" are optimized through backpropagation to find the continuous embedding that best steers the model for your task.

💡 How prompt tuning differs from prompt engineering

Prompt engineering searches over discrete tokens — you try different phrasings manually. Prompt tuning optimizes in continuous embedding space — gradient descent finds embeddings that may not correspond to any real token but are optimal for the task. The learned "soft prompt" is typically uninterpretable — it's a dense vector, not a readable sentence.

7.3 When These Work

Prompt tuning works surprisingly well for large models (>10B parameters), approaching full fine-tuning performance. For smaller models, it underperforms LoRA because there are fewer learnable parameters and the model has less capacity to be steered by input alone. The key finding from Lester et al.: as model size increases, the gap between prompt tuning and full fine-tuning shrinks to nearly zero.

9. Contrastive Fine-Tuning for Embeddings

All the methods above fine-tune for generative tasks — next-token prediction, classification, question answering. Embedding models require a fundamentally different approach: you're not generating text, you're shaping a vector space where similarity reflects relevance.

8.1 Contrastive Loss (InfoNCE)

InfoNCE Loss
$$\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(q, d^+) / \tau)}{\exp(\text{sim}(q, d^+) / \tau) + \sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\text{sim}(q, d_j^-) / \tau)}$$ where \(q\) is the query, \(d^+\) is the positive document, \(d_j^-\) are negatives, \(\tau\) is the temperature, and sim is typically cosine similarity.

The loss pulls the query and positive document embeddings together while pushing query and negative document embeddings apart. The denominator creates competition: the positive must score higher than all negatives, not just be "close enough."

8.2 Temperature: The Most Important Hyperparameter

Figure 8: Temperature controls the sharpness of the softmax in contrastive loss. Low temperature focuses learning on the hardest negatives; high temperature treats all negatives equally. The optimal temperature depends on negative quality — clean hard negatives benefit from low τ, noisy negatives need higher τ.

8.3 Negative Sampling Strategies

In-batch negatives: In a batch of B query-document pairs, each document serves as a negative for all other queries. You get B−1 free negatives per query with no extra computation. The downside: popular items appear as negatives disproportionately, potentially learning to suppress them (popularity bias). Fix: logQ correction — subtract log(item frequency) from scores.

Hard negative mining: Use the current model to find documents that score high but aren't relevant. These provide the strongest gradient signal — they're near the decision boundary. ANCE (Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation) mines hard negatives from the ANN index asynchronously. The risk: false negatives (the "hard negative" is actually relevant), which confuse training.

Multi-stage training: The modern recipe used by E5, GTE, and most strong embedding models: Stage 1 — train with random/in-batch negatives for broad alignment. Stage 2 — mine hard negatives with the Stage 1 model and fine-tune for precision. This avoids the cold-start problem of hard negative mining (you need a reasonable model to find meaningful hard negatives).

8.4 Contrastive Fine-Tuning + LoRA

LoRA works well with contrastive fine-tuning — you apply LoRA to the encoder and train with contrastive loss instead of cross-entropy. This is exactly what's done in many production retrieval systems: take a pretrained encoder (e.g., E5-base), apply LoRA to the attention layers, and fine-tune with domain-specific query-document pairs using InfoNCE loss. The result is a domain-adapted embedding model at a fraction of the compute cost of full fine-tuning.

10. Knowledge Distillation

📄
Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network Hinton, Vartol & Dean · NeurIPS Workshop 2015 — The foundational distillation paper

Knowledge distillation transfers the capabilities of a large teacher model into a smaller, faster student model. Unlike fine-tuning (which adapts a model's behavior), distillation creates a new, compact model that mimics the teacher — enabling deployment on devices where the teacher can't run.

Figure: Knowledge distillation. The teacher produces soft probability distributions (logits softened by temperature T). The student learns to match these soft labels (distillation loss) while also learning from ground-truth hard labels (task loss). The soft labels encode "dark knowledge" — relationships between classes that hard labels don't capture.

10.1 Why Soft Labels Carry More Information

A hard label says "this is class A." A teacher's soft prediction says "this is 90% class A, 7% class B, 2% class C, 1% class D." The 7% for class B is dark knowledge — it tells the student that B is more similar to A than D is. This structure in the label space provides a much richer training signal than one-hot labels, effectively giving the student more information per training example.

Distillation Loss
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}} = T^2 \cdot D_{\text{KL}}\!\left(\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{z_t}{T}\right) \;\Big\|\; \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{z_s}{T}\right)\right)$$ where \(z_t, z_s\) are teacher and student logits, and \(T\) is the distillation temperature. The \(T^2\) scaling compensates for the reduced gradient magnitudes when temperature is high.

10.2 Temperature in Distillation

Temperature \(T\) softens the probability distribution. At \(T=1\), you get the standard softmax — the teacher's top prediction dominates. At higher \(T\) (3–20), the distribution flattens, revealing more structure in the lower-probability classes. This is where the dark knowledge lives.

Low T (1–3): Student focuses on matching the teacher's top predictions. Better when the teacher is highly confident and the task is well-defined.

High T (5–20): Student learns more about inter-class relationships. Better for complex tasks where understanding similarity structure matters.

10.3 Logit-Level vs. Feature-Level Distillation

ApproachWhat the student learns fromWhen to use
Logit distillationTeacher's output probabilitiesClassification, ranking — teacher and student can have different architectures
Feature distillationTeacher's intermediate representationsWhen teacher and student share similar architecture; captures richer internal structure
Attention distillationTeacher's attention patternsTransformer-to-transformer; preserves learned attention behaviors

Feature-level distillation aligns intermediate representations between teacher and student layers: \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{feat}} = \|f_s(h_s) - h_t\|^2\), where \(f_s\) is a learned projection to match dimensions. TinyBERT uses both attention and feature distillation to compress BERT into a 4-layer model with minimal quality loss.

10.4 Distillation for Retrieval & Ranking

This is where distillation is most relevant to production search systems — and to your work:

Cross-encoder → Bi-encoder: Train a high-quality cross-encoder teacher (which processes query-document pairs jointly), then distill into a bi-encoder student (which encodes them independently). The student learns the teacher's relevance scores as soft labels. This is exactly what Pinterest, eBay, and Walmart use — a large LLM teacher produces 5-scale relevance labels offline, distilled into a lightweight model that runs online.

LLM-as-judge: Use a large LLM (e.g., GPT-4, LLaMA 8B) to label query-document relevance with chain-of-thought reasoning. These labels become soft targets for a smaller student ranker. The LLM never runs at inference — its knowledge is captured in the student.

💡 Why distillation matters for Apple

Apple's on-device models (3B parameters) can't match cloud models in raw capability. Distillation is how Apple transfers knowledge from large server-side models into on-device models — maintaining quality while meeting strict latency, memory, and privacy constraints. The Apple Intelligence Foundation Language Models report describes a multi-stage distillation pipeline from larger to smaller models. Being able to design a distillation pipeline is a critical skill for Apple ML roles.

10.5 Self-Distillation and Progressive Distillation

Self-distillation: The model distills from itself — use the model's predictions from a previous training stage as soft labels for the next stage. Surprisingly effective as a regularization technique even when not compressing the model.

Progressive distillation: Instead of distilling directly from a large teacher to a tiny student (which can be too large a gap), use intermediate-sized models: 70B → 13B → 3B → 1B. Each step is easier because the capacity gap is smaller.

11. Instruction Tuning

📄
Scaling Instruction-Finetuned Language Models (Flan-PaLM) Chung et al. · JMLR 2024 — Showed instruction tuning generalizes to unseen tasks

Instruction tuning is a specific form of SFT where the training data is formatted as (instruction, response) pairs across diverse tasks. The key finding: models trained on instructions for task A generalize to completely unseen task B — they learn to follow instructions, not just perform specific tasks.

Figure: Instruction tuning trains on diverse (instruction, response) pairs. The model learns to follow the format and intent of instructions, enabling generalization to unseen tasks at inference — the key capability behind chat models like ChatGPT and Claude.

11.1 What Makes Good Instruction Data

Diversity over volume. FLAN used 1,800+ tasks; Alpaca showed 52K examples across diverse instructions suffice for a strong chat model. The LIMA paper demonstrated that 1,000 carefully curated examples can produce a competitive model — confirming that instruction tuning is about teaching format and intent, not memorizing answers.

Quality signals: Well-structured instructions with clear expectations, responses that are helpful and accurate, and coverage of diverse task types (open-ended generation, classification, extraction, reasoning, coding). Synthetic data from stronger models (Alpaca's GPT-3.5 generations, Vicuna's ShareGPT data) works well as a starting point but has a quality ceiling bounded by the source model.

11.2 Instruction Tuning vs. Standard SFT

Standard SFT trains on a single task's data (e.g., NLI pairs for entailment classification). Instruction tuning trains on many tasks unified by an instruction format. The critical difference: instruction-tuned models can follow novel instructions at inference ("Explain X as if I'm five years old"), while standard SFT models only perform the specific task they were trained on. Instruction tuning is the bridge between "a language model that predicts next tokens" and "an assistant that follows user requests."

12. RLHF & Preference-Based Fine-Tuning

Standard fine-tuning optimizes for matching a reference output — the model learns to predict the next token given a context. But for chat models, there's no single "correct" output. Instead, we want models that produce outputs humans prefer. This requires a fundamentally different objective.

9.1 The RLHF Pipeline

Figure 9: The three-stage RLHF pipeline. SFT creates a reasonable starting model. The reward model learns human preferences. PPO optimizes the policy against the reward model while staying close to SFT (KL penalty prevents reward hacking).

Stage 1 — SFT: Supervised fine-tuning on high-quality demonstrations. This teaches the model the desired output format and establishes a baseline behavior. The SFT model becomes the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) for the RL stage.

Stage 2 — Reward model: Train a model on human preference pairs: given the same prompt and two responses, which does the human prefer? The reward model learns to assign a scalar score to any (prompt, response) pair.

Reward Model Loss (Bradley-Terry)
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} = -\log \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big)$$ where \(y_w\) is the preferred response, \(y_l\) is the rejected response, and \(r\) is the reward model's score. This is the same as binary cross-entropy on "which response is better?"

Stage 3 — PPO: Use the reward model to provide reward signal for reinforcement learning. The policy (LLM) generates responses, the reward model scores them, and PPO updates the policy to maximize reward while staying close to the SFT model via a KL divergence penalty:

PPO Objective
$$\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}[\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}}]$$ The KL penalty prevents reward hacking — without it, the model finds degenerate outputs that exploit the reward model's weaknesses (e.g., generating verbose, repetitive text that scores high on the reward model but is low quality).

9.2 DPO: Skipping the Reward Model

📄
Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model Rafailov et al. · NeurIPS 2023 — Eliminates the reward model and RL loop entirely

DPO's key insight: the optimal policy under the RLHF objective has a closed-form relationship with the reward function. This means you can reparameterize the reward in terms of the policy itself and optimize directly on preference pairs — no separate reward model, no PPO, no RL instabilities.

DPO Loss
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right)$$ The implicit reward is \(r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\). The policy is the reward model.

Why DPO is increasingly preferred: simpler pipeline (two stages instead of three), more stable training (no RL), lower compute cost (no reward model inference during training), and competitive or better performance in practice. The main limitation: DPO can be less robust when preference data is noisy or conflicting, because it has no explicit reward model to smooth over inconsistencies.

9.3 Newer Alternatives

SimPO — simplifies DPO further by using the average log probability as an implicit reward (no reference model needed). ORPO — combines SFT and preference optimization into a single stage using an odds ratio penalty. KTO — works with binary feedback (good/bad) instead of paired comparisons, which is easier to collect. All share the goal of making alignment simpler and more stable than RLHF.

13. Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization & Beyond

Fine-tuning adapts what a model does; compression adapts where it can run. For Apple — where models must execute on-device under tight latency, memory, and power budgets — compression is as important as fine-tuning itself.

13.1 Pruning

Remove weights or structures from the model to reduce size and computation:

Unstructured pruning zeros out individual weights based on magnitude (smallest weights → least important). Achieves high sparsity (90%+) with minimal accuracy loss, but sparse matrices don't map well to GPU hardware — you need specialized sparse kernels to get actual speedups. The lottery ticket hypothesis (Frankle & Carlin, 2019) showed that dense networks contain sparse subnetworks that can match full performance.

Structured pruning removes entire neurons, attention heads, or layers. The result is a standard dense model (just smaller), so you get real speedups on any hardware. Less aggressive compression ratios than unstructured, but immediately deployable. Pruning entire attention heads that contribute little to downstream tasks is a common technique.

Movement pruning (Sanh et al., 2020): Instead of pruning by weight magnitude, prune by how much weights change during fine-tuning. Weights that don't move are less important for the task — a more principled criterion for task-specific pruning.

13.2 Quantization

Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)After training, no retrainingSlight degradationQuick deployment; INT8 is nearly lossless

QAT inserts fake quantization nodes during training — the forward pass simulates INT8/INT4, but gradients flow through in full precision using the straight-through estimator. The model learns to be robust to quantization during training, producing higher quality than applying quantization after the fact.

14. Practical Considerations

14.1 How to Choose a Method

Figure 10: Decision tree for choosing a fine-tuning method. Start with data volume, then consider compute constraints, model size, and domain shift. In practice, LoRA is the default starting point — only move to full fine-tuning if LoRA underperforms and you have the compute budget.

14.2 Mixed Precision Training

Modern fine-tuning almost always uses mixed precision — different numerical formats for different operations:

FormatBitsRangeUsed for
FP3232±3.4 × 10³⁸Master weights, optimizer states, loss scaling
FP1616±65,504Forward/backward pass (with loss scaling)
BF1616±3.4 × 10³⁸Forward/backward pass (preferred for transformers)

Why BF16 over FP16 for transformers: FP16 has higher precision (more mantissa bits) but a tiny dynamic range — attention logits and loss values can overflow, causing NaN. BF16 matches FP32's range (same exponent bits) with less precision, which is almost always a better tradeoff for LLM training. If you see NaN losses during fine-tuning with FP16, switching to BF16 often fixes it.

Loss scaling (FP16 only): Small gradients can underflow to zero in FP16. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large factor (e.g., 1024) before backprop to keep gradients in representable range, then divides the gradients by the same factor before the optimizer step. Dynamic loss scaling automatically adjusts the scale factor. BF16 doesn't need loss scaling because its range matches FP32.

14.3 Gradient Accumulation

When your GPU can only fit a micro-batch of 2–4 examples but you need an effective batch size of 32–64 for stable training:

Gradient Accumulation
$$\text{Effective batch size} = \text{micro\_batch\_size} \times \text{accumulation\_steps} \times \text{num\_GPUs}$$ Accumulate gradients over N forward/backward passes, then call optimizer.step() once. Mathematically equivalent to a large batch (ignoring batch norm, which uses per-micro-batch stats).

This is essential for QLoRA — you're fine-tuning on a single GPU with limited memory, but contrastive learning and instruction tuning benefit from larger effective batch sizes. Set accumulation steps to achieve your target batch size without exceeding GPU memory. The tradeoff: training is N× slower per step (N forward passes per update), but each update is higher quality.

14.4 Data Quality Over Quantity

For fine-tuning, data quality matters far more than quantity. A few thousand high-quality, carefully curated examples often outperform millions of noisy ones. The LIMA paper showed that fine-tuning Llama-65B on just 1,000 carefully selected examples produced a competitive chat model — demonstrating that alignment is primarily about quality, not scale.

14.5 When Fine-Tuning Hurts

⚠️ The alignment tax

Fine-tuning on task-specific data can degrade general capabilities. A model fine-tuned for legal document analysis might become worse at math or coding. This is the "alignment tax" — and it's why modern practice uses LoRA (constrains the update subspace) or carefully blends task data with general instruction data during fine-tuning.

14.6 Learning Rate and Scheduler

Fine-tuning typically uses 10–100× smaller learning rates than pretraining, with cosine decay and a short warmup (5–10% of total steps). For LoRA specifically, slightly higher learning rates work (1e-4 to 3e-4) because the adapter parameters are initialized from scratch and need to move more than pretrained weights would.

15. Interview Questions

Q1 — Why does LoRA work? What's the theoretical justification?

Aghajanyan et al. (2021) showed that pretrained models have a low intrinsic dimensionality — the meaningful optimization during fine-tuning happens in a subspace far smaller than the full parameter space. LoRA exploits this by constraining the update to a rank-r subspace: ΔW = BA where B ∈ ℝ^(d×r) and A ∈ ℝ^(r×d). Since the true update is approximately low-rank, this decomposition captures most of the fine-tuning signal with orders of magnitude fewer parameters. Intuitively, pretraining already places the model near a good solution; fine-tuning makes a small, structured adjustment — and small adjustments in high-dimensional spaces tend to be low-rank.

Q2 — How do you choose the rank r in LoRA?

Rank controls the capacity of the adapter — higher rank = more expressiveness but with diminishing returns. Rules of thumb: r=4–8 for simple tasks (sentiment, classification), r=16 for moderate complexity, r=32–64 for complex tasks or large domain shifts. Empirically, performance improves steeply from r=1 to r=8, then plateaus. The right approach is to sweep over r={4, 8, 16, 32} and pick the smallest that matches your performance target — smaller rank means faster training, less memory, and easier multi-task serving. Also consider the task: if the task requires learning new factual knowledge (which lives in FFN layers), higher rank helps more than for tasks that only need to redirect existing capabilities.

Q3 — Explain QLoRA. Why doesn't 4-bit quantization hurt quality?

QLoRA stores the base model in 4-bit NormalFloat precision while keeping LoRA adapters in BF16. The key insight: you're not training the quantized weights — the base model is frozen. All learning happens in the full-precision adapters. The quantized base model acts as a fixed feature extractor; the quantization error is a constant approximation that the adapters compensate for. NF4 specifically places quantization levels at the quantiles of the normal distribution (which pretrained weights approximately follow), minimizing information loss. Additionally, double quantization compresses the quantization constants themselves, and paged optimizers handle memory spikes. The result: fine-tune a 65B model on a single 48GB GPU with no observable quality loss vs. 16-bit LoRA.

Q4 — LoRA vs. adapters — why did LoRA win?

The critical difference is inference latency. Adapters insert new sequential layers into the forward pass — every forward pass goes through extra bottleneck modules, adding latency. LoRA modifies existing weight matrices: at inference, compute W' = W₀ + BA once and replace the original weight. The model is architecturally identical to the original — zero added latency. Additionally, LoRA is more parameter-efficient (~0.1–1% vs. adapters' ~3.6%), and the merge-and-deploy story is simpler. Adapters still appear in some architectures (e.g., LLaVA's vision-language connector), but for standard fine-tuning, LoRA is the default.

Q5 — How does the temperature parameter affect contrastive learning?

Temperature τ controls the sharpness of the softmax in the InfoNCE loss. Low τ (0.01–0.03): the softmax becomes very peaked — gradients are dominated by the hardest negatives (those closest to the query). This gives strong learning signal but is sensitive to noisy/false negatives. High τ (0.5–1.0): the softmax is nearly uniform — all negatives contribute equally, giving weak but stable signal. Slow convergence, poor discrimination. The sweet spot (0.05–0.1): balanced signal from both hard and easy negatives. The optimal value depends on negative quality — if you have clean hard negatives (from a good mining pipeline), lower τ works well. If negatives are noisy (in-batch), higher τ adds robustness. Temperature can also be learned as a trainable parameter (as in CLIP).

Q6 — Explain DPO. Why is it preferred over RLHF?

DPO observes that the optimal policy under the RLHF objective has a closed-form relationship with the reward function: r(x,y) = β·log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)). This means you can directly optimize on preference pairs without training a separate reward model or running PPO. The loss pushes up the probability of preferred responses and down the probability of rejected ones, relative to the reference policy. Advantages: simpler pipeline (2 stages vs. 3), more stable training (no RL instabilities), lower compute (no reward model inference during training), and competitive or better performance. Limitation: DPO can be less robust with noisy preferences because it has no explicit reward model to smooth over inconsistencies — each preference pair directly updates the policy.

Q7 — You're fine-tuning an embedding model for enterprise search. Walk through your approach.

Start with a pretrained encoder (e.g., E5-base or a multilingual model). Apply LoRA to the attention layers (W_Q, W_V) with r=16. Data: Construct query-document pairs from click logs, user tickets, and LLM-generated synthetic queries for coverage. Use confidence-weighted positives (completed tickets > brief views). Negatives: Two-stage approach — first train with in-batch negatives (with logQ correction for popularity bias), then mine hard negatives from the ANN index and fine-tune a second stage. Loss: InfoNCE with temperature 0.05, in-batch negatives. Evaluation: Recall@50 offline on held-out queries, then A/B test measuring click-through rate and citation rate. Why LoRA over full fine-tuning: multiple connector types need separate adapters (ADO, JIRA, ServiceNow) — LoRA lets us share one base model and swap lightweight adapters per connector type.

Q8 — What is catastrophic forgetting and how do different methods address it?

Catastrophic forgetting occurs when gradient updates for a new task overwrite representations learned during pretraining. A model fine-tuned on medical text might lose its ability to do math. Different methods address it differently: Full fine-tuning — most susceptible; mitigate with small learning rate, short training, or mixing task data with general data. LoRA — constrains updates to a low-rank subspace, limiting how much the model can deviate from pretrained weights. The frozen base model is always intact. Adapters — similar benefit; frozen backbone preserves pretraining. Prefix/prompt tuning — doesn't modify any weights at all, only adds virtual tokens. Zero forgetting by construction, but limited expressiveness. The general principle: the more parameters you freeze, the less forgetting risk but the less adaptation capacity.

Q9 — When would you choose full fine-tuning over LoRA?

Full fine-tuning when: (1) large domain shift — target domain is fundamentally different from pretraining (e.g., a specialized scientific or legal domain with unique vocabulary and reasoning patterns), (2) abundant high-quality data — 50K+ examples, enough to update all parameters without overfitting, (3) sufficient compute — you can afford multi-GPU training with full optimizer states, (4) single task — you only need one model, so storing a full copy is fine, (5) LoRA underperforms — you've tried LoRA with high rank and it doesn't match the quality you need. In practice, always try LoRA first — it's cheaper, faster, and often sufficient. Move to full fine-tuning only when you've verified the quality gap is real and worth the compute cost.

Q10 — How does AdamW differ from L2 regularization with Adam? Why does it matter for fine-tuning?

Standard L2 adds the weight penalty to the loss: ∇(L + λ||w||²). The gradient of the penalty (2λw) flows through Adam's adaptive moment estimates — Adam's per-parameter scaling can suppress the regularization effect for parameters with large gradients. AdamW decouples weight decay: it subtracts λw directly from weights after the Adam step, bypassing the adaptive scaling. This means every parameter decays at the same rate regardless of gradient magnitude. For fine-tuning specifically, this matters because you want consistent regularization across all parameters to prevent the model from drifting too far from pretrained weights — AdamW's decoupled decay gives you that uniform pull toward smaller weights.

Q11 — Walk through how you'd design a distillation pipeline for on-device search ranking.

Teacher: Train a cross-encoder teacher (e.g., 8B LLM) that jointly encodes query-document pairs for maximum relevance quality. Run the teacher offline on a large corpus of query-document pairs to produce graded relevance scores (1–5 scale). Student: A lightweight model (bi-encoder or late-interaction, ~100M params) that must run in <10ms on-device. Distillation: Train the student to match the teacher's relevance scores using MSE or KL divergence on the score distributions. Also include hard label cross-entropy loss on click data for grounding. Compression: After distillation, apply structured pruning (remove low-contribution attention heads), then quantize to INT8 using QAT (quantization-aware training during the final training stage). Evaluation: Compare student vs. teacher on offline NDCG@10, then A/B test online measuring latency, CTR, and user satisfaction. Target: within 95% of teacher quality at 100× throughput.

Q12 — When would you use domain-adaptive pretraining vs. going straight to task fine-tuning?

Use DAPT when the target domain has specialized vocabulary, conventions, or reasoning patterns poorly represented in the base model's pretraining data — legal, medical, scientific, enterprise IT. Gururangan et al. (2020) showed DAPT consistently improves downstream performance even for domains that seem well-covered by web text. Skip DAPT when: (1) the domain is well-represented in pretraining (general web Q&A, common topics), (2) you don't have access to enough domain text (DAPT needs at least a few million tokens to be effective), or (3) the model is already domain-adapted (using BioBERT for biomedical tasks). A practical signal: if your model makes basic vocabulary errors on domain text (misunderstanding abbreviations, jargon, domain-specific phrasing), DAPT will help. DAPT is also cheap — it's unsupervised, so you only need raw domain text, not labels.

Q13 — Explain the difference between post-training quantization and quantization-aware training.

PTQ quantizes a fully-trained FP32/FP16 model to INT8/INT4 after training is complete. No retraining needed — just calibrate quantization ranges using a small representative dataset. Fast and simple, but quality can degrade at aggressive quantization (INT4). Works well for INT8, which is nearly lossless for most models. QAT inserts simulated quantization operations into the training graph. During forward pass, weights and activations are quantized and dequantized (using the straight-through estimator for gradients). The model learns to produce outputs that are robust to quantization noise. Higher quality than PTQ at the same bit width, especially at INT4, but requires retraining and is more complex. When to use which: PTQ for quick INT8 deployment. QAT when PTQ quality at your target bit width is unacceptable, or for INT4 deployment where quality matters.

Q14 — Why is BF16 preferred over FP16 for transformer training?

BF16 and FP16 both use 16 bits, but allocate them differently. FP16 has 5 exponent bits and 10 mantissa bits — high precision but a small dynamic range (max ~65K). BF16 has 8 exponent bits and 7 mantissa bits — less precision but the same dynamic range as FP32 (max ~3.4×10³⁸). In transformers, attention logits and loss values can grow large (especially with long sequences or early unstable training), overflowing FP16's range and producing NaN. BF16's larger range handles this naturally without loss scaling. The reduced precision (7 vs. 10 mantissa bits) rarely matters — transformer training is more sensitive to range than precision. That's why BF16 is the default for training on A100/H100 GPUs, and FP16 requires careful loss scaling to avoid numerical issues.

Q15 — What is instruction tuning and why does it enable zero-shot generalization?

Instruction tuning fine-tunes a model on diverse (instruction, response) pairs across many tasks — summarization, translation, QA, coding, reasoning — all formatted with natural language instructions. The key insight: the model doesn't just learn each task individually; it learns to follow the instruction format itself. This meta-learning effect means it can generalize to unseen tasks described in the same instruction format. FLAN showed this at scale: training on 1,800+ tasks significantly improved performance on held-out tasks the model never saw during training. The LIMA paper then showed quality matters more than quantity — 1,000 carefully curated examples can produce a competitive instruction-following model, suggesting that instruction tuning teaches format compliance and intent recognition, not task-specific knowledge.
